1. Fossil Evidence & Geological Dating Paleontology
  • Multiple Independent Lines of Evidence: Evolution is supported by converging data from geology, morphology, molecular biochemistry, genetics, biogeography, and mathematical modeling showing that organisms have changed over time and share common ancestry.
  • What Fossils Reveal: Document extinct species, chronological succession of life forms, and transitional fossils that bridge major taxonomic groups.
  • Two Methods of Dating Fossils:
    • Relative Dating (Sedimentary Strata): Uses the Law of Superposition — in undisturbed sedimentary rock layers, deeper strata = older and upper strata = younger.
    • Radiometric (Absolute) Dating: Measures the constant exponential decay of radioactive parent isotopes into stable daughter isotopes using known half-lives (t₁/₂):
      • Carbon-14 (¹⁴C → ¹⁴N, t₁/₂ = 5,730 yr): Dates young organic remains up to ~50,000–75,000 years old.
      • Uranium-238 (²³⁸U → ²⁰⁶Pb, t₁/₂ = 4.5 billion yr) / Potassium-40: Dates ancient volcanic rock layers bracketing older fossils.
Famous Transitional Fossils:
  • Tiktaalik roseae (~375 mya): Transitional lobe-finned fish → tetrapod with gills/scales AND flat skull, neck, ribs, and wrist-like fin bones.
  • Archaeopteryx (~150 mya): Transitional non-avian dinosaur → bird with flight feathers/wishbone AND teeth, clawed wings, and bony tail.
  • Pakicetus / Ambulocetus: Land artiodactyls → modern whales (hind limb reduction).
Rock layers = timeline of Earth history.
2. Geographical Evidence (Biogeography) Continental Drift & Islands
  • Biogeography: The scientific study of the past and present geographic distribution of species across continents and islands.
  • Plate Tectonics & Continental Drift (Pangaea → Gondwana/Laurasia):
    • Similar fossils (e.g., Glossopteris ferns, Cynognathus reptiles) and living flightless ratite birds (ostrich, rhea, emu) are found on separated southern continents that were once joined.
    • Geographic isolation via continental breakup reduced gene flow and exposed populations to different selective pressures, driving divergence.
  • Island Endemics: Oceanic islands (e.g., Galápagos, Hawaii) are colonized by species from the nearest mainland, which then diversify via adaptive radiation.
3. Vestigial Structures & Comparative Embryology Developmental Homologies
  • Vestigial Structures: Reduced, degenerate, or nonfunctional anatomical remnants inherited from a functional structure in an ancestor.
    • Examples: Pelvic girdle & femur bones inside baleen whales and boa constrictors; blind eye remnants in cavefish; human coccyx (tailbone) and appendix.
  • Comparative Embryology: Early vertebrate embryos share conserved developmental stages — pharyngeal pouches (gill slits) and a post-anal tail — demonstrating shared inherited developmental gene programs (e.g., Hox genes).
4. Comparative Anatomy: Homologous vs. Analogous Divergent vs. Convergent
Feature Homologous Structures Analogous Structures
Definition Similar underlying anatomy/development inherited from a common ancestor (may have different functions). Similar superficial function/shape in unrelated lineages due to similar environmental pressures.
Evolutionary Pattern Divergent Evolution (e.g., mammalian pentadactyl forelimbs) Convergent Evolution (e.g., insect wing vs. bat wing; shark vs. dolphin)
Phylogenetic Use? YES — used to construct cladograms & infer ancestry. NO (Homoplasy) — can mislead morphological trees!
5. Molecular Homologies: DNA & Protein Comparisons Strongest Evidence
  • DNA, RNA, & Amino Acid Sequence Alignment: Provides the most quantitative, objective measure of evolutionary relatedness.
    • Fewer nucleotide/amino acid differences = more recent common ancestor.
    • More sequence differences = more time has elapsed since lineages diverged.
  • Highly Conserved Proteins: Essential proteins like Cytochrome c (mitochondrial ETC), Hemoglobin, and Histones are present across diverse species; sequence comparisons map deep evolutionary relationships.
  • Molecular Clocks: Neutral mutations accumulate at a relatively constant rate over time, allowing scientists to estimate the time of divergence between two clades.
AP Data Table Skill: When given a matrix of "Number of Amino Acid Differences in Cytochrome c" (or "% DNA Sequence Identity"), remember: 0–1 differences = closest sister taxa; largest number of differences = outgroup (diverged earliest)!

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 1, 2 & 3 (Artificial Selection, Hardy-Weinberg Mathematical Modeling & BLAST Bioinformatics): Comparing allele frequency shifts across generations under directional selection vs. small-population genetic drift simulations, and constructing maximum-parsimony cladograms from BLAST percentage sequence identity matrices (Cytochrome c / β-globin). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Golden Rule: Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE): Allele frequencies p + q = 1; Genotype frequencies p² (homozygous dominant) + 2pq (heterozygous carriers) + q² (homozygous recessive) = 1. Golden Calculation Sequence: ALWAYS begin with the recessive phenotype frequency (q²), take √(q²) to find q, compute p = 1 − q, and then calculate 2pq (heterozygote carrier frequency). Never take the square root of the dominant phenotype frequency (p² + 2pq)! Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Evolutionary & Biomedical Disruption Case Study: Incomplete antibiotic regimens or agricultural antibiotic overuse exert strong directional selection on bacterial populations (e.g., MRSA or MDR-Tuberculosis): susceptible cells die while rare, pre-existing resistant mutants (arising via random mutation or R-plasmid conjugation) survive and reproduce. In conservation biology, severe population bottlenecks (e.g., Northern Elephant Seals, African Cheetahs) drastically erode genetic diversity via genetic drift, increasing homozygosity for deleterious recessive alleles and vulnerability to novel pathogens.
