Natural Selection as a Major Mechanism of Evolution Core Mechanism
  • Natural selection is the primary non-random mechanism of adaptive evolution first articulated by Charles Darwin & Alfred Russel Wallace.
  • It changes the genetic composition of populations over generations when certain heritable traits increase survival and/or reproductive success in a specific environment.
  • Over generations, favorable traits (adaptations) become more common in the population, while unfavorable traits become less common.
Individuals do NOT evolve; populations do! An individual organism cannot alter its own genotype to adapt during its lifetime — evolution is the change in allele frequencies in a population across generations.
What Natural Selection Requires (Darwin's 4 Postulates) Prerequisites
  • 1. Overproduction & Competition for Limited Resources: Organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support (N > K). Individuals compete for scarce food, water, shelter, territory, and mates.
  • 2. Phenotypic Variation: Individuals within a population exhibit diverse traits (arising from random mutations, crossing over, independent assortment, and random fertilization).
  • 3. Heritability of Traits: Trait differences must have a genetic basis (DNA alleles) so they can be passed from parents to offspring.
  • 4. Differential Survival & Reproduction: Because resources are limited, individuals with phenotypes better suited to the environment survive at higher rates and leave more fertile offspring.
Natural selection begins when there are more individuals than resources. Competition for limited resources causes differential survival and reproduction!
Darwin’s Core Idea: Differential Survival & Reproduction Allele Shifts
Heritable Variation → Resource Competition → Differential Reproduction → Adaptation Increases
  • Individuals with more favorable phenotypes are more likely to:
    • Survive better (evade predators, acquire nutrients, tolerate stress)
    • Reproduce more (attract mates, produce higher viable fecundity)
    • Pass on their advantageous alleles to the next generation
  • How Natural Selection Affects Populations: Favorable alleles increase in frequency (Δp > 0) while less favorable phenotypes/alleles decrease over time.
Helpful traits stick around because their carriers leave more offspring.

Evolutionary Fitness & Adaptation Reproductive Success
  • Evolutionary Fitness = Reproductive Success: Measured strictly by the relative number of viable, fertile offspring an individual contributes to the next generation's gene pool.
    • A "more fit" organism leaves more surviving offspring than competitors in the same environment.
    • Fitness is always relative to the current environment and context-dependent.
  • Adaptation: Any heritable trait (morphological, physiological, or behavioral) that increases an organism's evolutionary fitness in a particular environment.
Living longer, being stronger, or being bigger does NOT always mean higher fitness! A sterile mule or a 100-year-old tortoise that leaves 0 offspring has an evolutionary fitness of zero (w = 0).

Environment Determines What Is Favorable Biotic & Abiotic Pressures
  • A trait is beneficial only in a specific environmental context. If the environment shifts, a previously advantageous trait may become neutral or deleterious, and a different trait may be favored.
  • Biotic Factors (Living Selection Pressures):
    • Examples: Predators, interspecific/intraspecific competitors, pathogens/parasites, mate preferences, and mutualists (pollinators).
  • Abiotic Factors (Nonliving Selection Pressures):
    • Examples: Temperature extremes, water availability/drought, sunlight intensity, soil pH/mineral nutrients, salinity, and dissolved oxygen.
Rock Pocket Mice (Chaetodipus intermedius): Light sandy fur provides high fitness on light desert granite, but after volcanic eruptions created dark basalt lava flows, the Mc1r dark fur mutation became strongly favored on dark rock due to owl predation.

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 1, 2 & 3 (Artificial Selection, Hardy-Weinberg Mathematical Modeling & BLAST Bioinformatics): Comparing allele frequency shifts across generations under directional selection vs. small-population genetic drift simulations, and constructing maximum-parsimony cladograms from BLAST percentage sequence identity matrices (Cytochrome c / β-globin). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Golden Rule: Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE): Allele frequencies p + q = 1; Genotype frequencies p² (homozygous dominant) + 2pq (heterozygous carriers) + q² (homozygous recessive) = 1. Golden Calculation Sequence: ALWAYS begin with the recessive phenotype frequency (q²), take √(q²) to find q, compute p = 1 − q, and then calculate 2pq (heterozygote carrier frequency). Never take the square root of the dominant phenotype frequency (p² + 2pq)! Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Evolutionary & Biomedical Disruption Case Study: Incomplete antibiotic regimens or agricultural antibiotic overuse exert strong directional selection on bacterial populations (e.g., MRSA or MDR-Tuberculosis): susceptible cells die while rare, pre-existing resistant mutants (arising via random mutation or R-plasmid conjugation) survive and reproduce. In conservation biology, severe population bottlenecks (e.g., Northern Elephant Seals, African Cheetahs) drastically erode genetic diversity via genetic drift, increasing homozygosity for deleterious recessive alleles and vulnerability to novel pathogens.
