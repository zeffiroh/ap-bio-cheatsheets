What Is Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE)? Null Hypothesis Model
  • Theoretical Null Model: Mathematical model describing a hypothetical non-evolving population in which allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation.
  • Purpose of the Model: Acts as a baseline "control group" (null hypothesis H₀) for population genetics:
    • Scientists compare observed genotype frequencies in nature against expected Hardy-Weinberg frequencies (often using a Chi-Square χ² test).
    • Real populations rarely meet all conditions; significant deviations indicate that one or more evolutionary forces are actively acting on the population!
Hardy–Weinberg = "Control Group" for Evolution. If observed ≠ expected frequencies → evolution is occurring!
The 5 Conditions Required for Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium Must Memorize All 5
HWE Condition Evolutionary Force Prevented Effect If Violated
1. Extremely Large Population Size Prevents Genetic Drift Small N causes random chance fluctuations in p and q.
2. No Migration (Isolation) Prevents Gene Flow Immigration/emigration adds or removes alleles from the gene pool.
3. No Net Mutations Prevents Mutational Shift New alleles arise or convert one allele into another (A → a).
4. Random Mating Prevents Assortative Mating / Inbreeding Alters genotype frequencies first (e.g., inbreeding increases p² & q², decreases 2pq).
5. No Natural Selection Prevents Differential Survival / Fitness All genotypes have equal fitness (w = 1); selection shifts both genotype & allele frequencies.
Non-random mating (e.g., self-fertilization or inbreeding) changes GENOTYPE frequencies (p², 2pq, q²) first, NOT allele frequencies (p, q) directly! Sexual selection (differential mating success), however, does alter allele frequencies.
The Two Hardy–Weinberg Equations (AP Formula Sheet) Alleles vs. Genotypes
1. Allele Frequencies: p + q = 1   |   2. Genotype Frequencies: p² + 2pq + q² = 1
  • Single-letter variables = ALLELE frequencies (gamete pool, total = 1.0 or 100%):
    • p = frequency of the dominant allele (A)
    • q = frequency of the recessive allele (a)
  • Squared/Product terms = GENOTYPE (individual) frequencies (diploid individuals, total = 1.0):
    • p² = frequency of homozygous dominant (AA) individuals
    • 2pq = frequency of heterozygous (Aa) carriers
    • q² = frequency of homozygous recessive (aa) individuals (equals recessive phenotype frequency!)
  • Dominant Phenotype Frequency: p² + 2pq (or 1 − q²). Never set dominant phenotype equal to p² alone!

Step-by-Step HWE Problem-Solving Golden Rule AP Math Workflow
1. Find q² (aa phenotype) → 2. q = √(q²) → 3. p = 1 − q → 4. Compute p² & 2pq
  • Golden Rule: When given phenotype counts, ALWAYS start with the homozygous recessive phenotype (q²) because it is the only phenotype whose genotype is known for certain!
  • If given dominant phenotype % (e.g., 84% dominant), subtract from 100% first to get recessive phenotype (q² = 1 − 0.84 = 0.16).
Worked AP Calculation Problem:
In a population of 500 wildflowers in HWE, 80 plants have white flowers (recessive, aa) and 420 have red flowers (dominant, AA or Aa). Find all allele & genotype frequencies and the number of heterozygous plants:
  • Step 1 (Find q²): q² = 80 / 500 = 0.16 (homozygous recessive genotype frequency)
  • Step 2 (Find q): q = √0.16 = 0.40 (recessive allele frequency)
  • Step 3 (Find p): p = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60 (dominant allele frequency)
  • Step 4 (Find Genotypes): p² = (0.60)² = 0.36 (AA)  |  2pq = 2(0.60)(0.40) = 0.48 (Aa carriers)
  • Step 5 (Number of Heterozygotes): 0.48 × 500 = 240 heterozygous (Aa) plants!
Common AP Math Trap: Read carefully whether the question asks for "frequency of the recessive allele" (q) or "frequency of the homozygous recessive genotype/phenotype" (q²) — and whether it wants a decimal frequency (0.48), percentage (48%), or headcount (240 individuals)!

Scholarly Dimension Comprehensive Academic Reference & Mechanistic Specification
I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 1, 2 & 3 (Artificial Selection, Hardy-Weinberg Mathematical Modeling & BLAST Bioinformatics): Comparing allele frequency shifts across generations under directional selection vs. small-population genetic drift simulations, and constructing maximum-parsimony cladograms from BLAST percentage sequence identity matrices (Cytochrome c / β-globin). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Golden Rule: Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE): Allele frequencies p + q = 1; Genotype frequencies p² (homozygous dominant) + 2pq (heterozygous carriers) + q² (homozygous recessive) = 1. Golden Calculation Sequence: ALWAYS begin with the recessive phenotype frequency (q²), take √(q²) to find q, compute p = 1 − q, and then calculate 2pq (heterozygote carrier frequency). Never take the square root of the dominant phenotype frequency (p² + 2pq)! Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Evolutionary & Biomedical Disruption Case Study: Incomplete antibiotic regimens or agricultural antibiotic overuse exert strong directional selection on bacterial populations (e.g., MRSA or MDR-Tuberculosis): susceptible cells die while rare, pre-existing resistant mutants (arising via random mutation or R-plasmid conjugation) survive and reproduce. In conservation biology, severe population bottlenecks (e.g., Northern Elephant Seals, African Cheetahs) drastically erode genetic diversity via genetic drift, increasing homozygosity for deleterious recessive alleles and vulnerability to novel pathogens.
