What Is Artificial Selection? Human-Directed Evolution
  • Artificial Selection (Selective Breeding): An evolutionary process in which humans intentionally choose which individual organisms reproduce based on desirable heritable phenotypes.
  • Key Features of Artificial Selection:
    • Intentional: Humans define the selection criteria (yield, size, temperament, aesthetics) rather than natural environmental survival.
    • Trait-Focused: Only individuals expressing desired phenotypes are allowed to breed.
    • Non-Random Mating: Controlled cross-breeding or inbreeding concentrates target alleles.
    • Rapid Change: Produces dramatic phenotypic divergence in far fewer generations than natural selection because humans eliminate waiting for slow environmental pressures.
Classic Examples of Artificial Selection:
  • Agriculture: Wild teosinte grass bred into modern high-yield corn (Zea mays); crops bred for drought tolerance and pest resistance.
  • Animal Breeding: Grey wolves (Canis lupus) domesticated into hundreds of dog breeds (Chihuahua to Great Dane) varying in size, coat, and herding/retrieving behavior; livestock bred for milk or meat yield.
  • Horticulture: Ornamental plants bred for flower color, size, and symmetry.
Genetic Consequences & Trade-Offs of Artificial Selection Diversity & Inbreeding
  • Increases Frequency of Desired Alleles: Rapidly drives human-favored alleles toward fixation (p → 1.0) while removing undesired alleles from the gene pool.
  • Reduces Overall Genetic Diversity: Because only a small subset of individuals is bred each generation, genetic variation across the genome drops sharply (artificial bottleneck).
  • Inbreeding Depression & Deleterious Recessives: Repeated mating among close relatives increases homozygosity (q²↑), exposing harmful recessive alleles that hitchhike alongside desired traits.
  • Monoculture Vulnerability: Genetically uniform crops lack the genetic variation needed to resist novel pathogens or pests.
Trade-Offs in Purebred Dogs: Selective breeding for physical traits has inadvertently concentrated harmful recessive disorders — e.g., hip dysplasia in German Shepherds, breathing problems in brachycephalic Bulldogs, and deafness in Dalmatians.
Convergent Evolution: Environmental Similarity in Nature EVO-1.G Connection
  • Just as humans can select for similar traits across different breeds, similar environmental selective pressures in nature select for similar adaptations in distantly related lineages (convergent evolution).
  • Result: Unrelated species independently evolve analogous structures that perform the same ecological function without sharing a recent common ancestor with that trait.
Australian Marsupials vs. Placental Mammals: Sugar gliders (marsupial) and flying squirrels (placental) independently evolved gliding skin membranes because both occupy similar arboreal forest niches.

Feature Artificial Selection Natural Selection
Selective Agent Humans (breeders, farmers, researchers) Environment (predators, climate, competition, disease)
Traits Favored Human-desired traits (yield, aesthetics, docility) — may reduce wild survival fitness Adaptations that strictly increase survival and reproductive fitness in nature
Rate of Change Often very rapid (visible changes in a few generations via strict breeding control) Usually gradual (depends on strength of environmental selective pressure)

Darwin's Analogy: Darwin used artificial selection in fancy pigeons, dogs, and crops as experimental proof that selection can dramatically reshape species over time — if humans can do it in decades, nature can do far more over millions of years!

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 1, 2 & 3 (Artificial Selection, Hardy-Weinberg Mathematical Modeling & BLAST Bioinformatics): Comparing allele frequency shifts across generations under directional selection vs. small-population genetic drift simulations, and constructing maximum-parsimony cladograms from BLAST percentage sequence identity matrices (Cytochrome c / β-globin). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Golden Rule: Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE): Allele frequencies p + q = 1; Genotype frequencies p² (homozygous dominant) + 2pq (heterozygous carriers) + q² (homozygous recessive) = 1. Golden Calculation Sequence: ALWAYS begin with the recessive phenotype frequency (q²), take √(q²) to find q, compute p = 1 − q, and then calculate 2pq (heterozygote carrier frequency). Never take the square root of the dominant phenotype frequency (p² + 2pq)! Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Evolutionary & Biomedical Disruption Case Study: Incomplete antibiotic regimens or agricultural antibiotic overuse exert strong directional selection on bacterial populations (e.g., MRSA or MDR-Tuberculosis): susceptible cells die while rare, pre-existing resistant mutants (arising via random mutation or R-plasmid conjugation) survive and reproduce. In conservation biology, severe population bottlenecks (e.g., Northern Elephant Seals, African Cheetahs) drastically erode genetic diversity via genetic drift, increasing homozygosity for deleterious recessive alleles and vulnerability to novel pathogens.
