Selection Acts on Phenotypes (Not Directly on DNA) Genotype × Environment
  • Natural selection acts directly on phenotypes (observable physical, biochemical, or behavioral traits), which indirectly changes underlying genotype and allele frequencies in the gene pool.
  • Phenotype = Genotype + Environment: Traits that interact with the environment determine survival and reproduction.
  • Sources of Variation: Genetic variation (random mutations, meiotic recombination/crossing over, independent assortment) + environmental influences.
  • Phenotypic Plasticity: The ability of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes under different environmental conditions. Helps short-term survival, but because it is not a genetic change, plasticity does not directly cause evolution.
Phenotype–Environment Interaction: Poor childhood nutrition leads to reduced adult height despite inheriting alleles for tall stature; Arctic foxes change coat color from brown (summer) to white (winter) via phenotypic plasticity.
No variation = nothing to "select" from! Without heritable phenotypic variation, natural selection cannot occur.
Common Mistakes:
  • Assuming all individuals in a population respond the same to a selection pressure.
  • Organisms don't "try" to adapt! A giraffe doesn't stretch its neck and pass that on. Individuals with longer necks already existed; they just survived and reproduced more.
Molecular Variation Within Cells & Adaptability Cellular Level
  • Molecular Variation: Variation occurs at the cellular and molecular level in enzymes, pigments, membrane receptors, and surface proteins.
  • Fitness Impact: Different molecular variants can improve metabolic efficiency across temperatures, enhance pathogen protection, or confer toxin resistance.
  • Genetic Variation Increases Adaptability: Populations with greater molecular and genetic variation have a wider range of possible responses to new selection pressures and higher chances of long-term survival.
Lactase Persistence: Regulatory mutations that keep the LCT lactase enzyme gene expressed in adult humans provided a major nutritional and hydration advantage in pastoralist dairy-farming populations.
Genetic variation = biological toolbox for survival.
Sexual Selection & Heterozygote Advantage Special Selection Modes
  • Sexual Selection: Natural selection for traits that increase mating success, often producing pronounced sexual dimorphism:
    • Intrasexual Selection: Competition within the same sex (usually male-male combat, e.g., elk antlers, elephant seal territorial fights).
    • Intersexual Selection (Mate Choice): One sex (usually females) chooses mates based on showy ornaments or displays (e.g., peacock tail feathers, bird songs) that signal "good genes" despite predation trade-offs.
  • Heterozygote Advantage (Balancing Selection): When the heterozygous genotype (Aa) has higher fitness than either homozygous genotype (AA or aa), maintaining both alleles in the population.
Sickle-Cell Trait (HbA/HbS) in Malaria Regions:
  • HbA/HbA (Normal): Susceptible to fatal Plasmodium malaria infection.
  • HbS/HbS (Sickle-cell disease): Severe anemia / lower survival.
  • HbA/HbS (Heterozygote): Highest fitness — resistant to malaria without severe sickle-cell disease!

Selection Mode Mechanism & Distribution Effect Classic AP Biology Examples
Directional Selection Favors phenotypes at one extreme; shifts population mean toward that extreme (common when environment changes). • Peppered moths (Biston betularia): Shift from light to dark melanic form during Industrial Revolution soot pollution.
• Antibiotic/pesticide resistance & Darwin's finch deeper beaks during drought.
Stabilizing Selection Favors intermediate phenotypes and selects against both extremes; narrows distribution width and reduces genetic variance. • Human birth weight (~3.2–3.6 kg): Very low-weight babies suffer higher mortality; very large babies cause birth complications.
• Bird clutch size: Too few eggs = few offspring; too many = chicks starve.
Disruptive (Diversifying) Selection Favors both extremes over intermediate phenotypes; creates a bimodal distribution and can drive sympatric divergence. • Black-bellied seedcrackers: Small beaks crack soft seeds efficiently; large beaks crack hard seeds; intermediate beaks are inefficient at both.
Graph Interpretation Tip: On AP MCQs, compare the dashed line (before) to the solid line (after): if the peak moves sideways → Directional; if the peak gets taller and narrower → Stabilizing; if the peak splits into two humps → Disruptive!

Scholarly Dimension Comprehensive Academic Reference & Mechanistic Specification
I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 1, 2 & 3 (Artificial Selection, Hardy-Weinberg Mathematical Modeling & BLAST Bioinformatics): Comparing allele frequency shifts across generations under directional selection vs. small-population genetic drift simulations, and constructing maximum-parsimony cladograms from BLAST percentage sequence identity matrices (Cytochrome c / β-globin). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Golden Rule: Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE): Allele frequencies p + q = 1; Genotype frequencies p² (homozygous dominant) + 2pq (heterozygous carriers) + q² (homozygous recessive) = 1. Golden Calculation Sequence: ALWAYS begin with the recessive phenotype frequency (q²), take √(q²) to find q, compute p = 1 − q, and then calculate 2pq (heterozygote carrier frequency). Never take the square root of the dominant phenotype frequency (p² + 2pq)! Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Evolutionary & Biomedical Disruption Case Study: Incomplete antibiotic regimens or agricultural antibiotic overuse exert strong directional selection on bacterial populations (e.g., MRSA or MDR-Tuberculosis): susceptible cells die while rare, pre-existing resistant mutants (arising via random mutation or R-plasmid conjugation) survive and reproduce. In conservation biology, severe population bottlenecks (e.g., Northern Elephant Seals, African Cheetahs) drastically erode genetic diversity via genetic drift, increasing homozygosity for deleterious recessive alleles and vulnerability to novel pathogens.
