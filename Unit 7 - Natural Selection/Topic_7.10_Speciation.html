1. Biological Species Concept & Reproductive Isolation Definition of Species
  • Speciation: The evolutionary process by which one ancestral species splits into two or more reproductively isolated descendant species.
  • Biological Species Concept (Ernst Mayr): Defines a species as a group of populations whose members have the potential to interbreed in nature and produce viable, fertile offspring, and cannot produce viable, fertile offspring with other such groups.
    • Limitations: Does not apply to asexual prokaryotes, extinct fossils, or ring species (where morphological/phylogenetic/ecological species concepts are used instead).
  • Reproductive Isolation Is Key: Prevents gene flow between two populations, allowing their gene pools to diverge independently via mutation, natural selection, and genetic drift.
No gene flow = populations diverge genetically into separate species.
3-Step FRQ Template for "How Do Two Populations Become Separate Species?":
(1) Gene flow stops (geographic barrier or sympatric shift); (2) Different selective pressures, random mutations, and genetic drift act independently on each isolated population; (3) Enough genetic divergence accumulates to establish prezygotic or postzygotic reproductive isolation so they can no longer interbreed!
2. Prezygotic vs. Postzygotic Reproductive Barriers 8 Isolation Mechanisms
Barrier Type Specific Mechanism Classic Biological Example
PREZYGOTIC
(Before zygote forms; prevents mating or fertilization)		 1. Habitat Isolation Live in different microhabitats in same region (e.g., water-dwelling vs. terrestrial garter snakes; ground vs. tree frogs).
2. Temporal Isolation Breed at different times of day, seasons, or years (e.g., Eastern vs. Western spotted skunks breed in late winter vs. late summer).
3. Behavioral Isolation Incompatible courtship displays, songs, dances, or pheromones (e.g., blue-footed booby high-step dance; firefly flash patterns).
4. Mechanical Isolation Anatomically incompatible reproductive structures/genitalia or flower shapes (e.g., snail shell coil chirality; Salvia flowers).
5. Gametic Isolation Sperm cannot recognize or fuse with egg surface receptors (e.g., red vs. purple sea urchin bindin proteins in broadcast spawning).
POSTZYGOTIC
(After fertilization; prevents hybrid from becoming viable/fertile adult)		 6. Reduced Hybrid Viability Hybrid zygote fails to develop or dies frail before maturity (e.g., Ensatina salamander subspecies hybrids).
7. Reduced Hybrid Fertility Hybrid grows into a healthy adult but is sterile due to uneven chromosome pairing in meiosis (e.g., Mule [2n=63] = Horse [2n=64] × Donkey [2n=62]).
8. Hybrid Breakdown First-generation (F₁) hybrids are viable & fertile, but F₂ offspring are weak or sterile (e.g., cultivated rice strains).
3. Allopatric vs. Sympatric Speciation With vs. Without Geography
  • Allopatric Speciation ("Other Country"): Caused by geographic isolation (mountain range, canyon, river, isthmus, island colonization) physically separating a population and blocking gene flow.
    • Examples: Kaibab vs. Abert's squirrels on opposite rims of the Grand Canyon; snapping shrimp across the Isthmus of Panama.
  • Sympatric Speciation ("Same Country"): Occurs without geographic isolation while populations live in the exact same area, driven by:
    • 1. Polyploidy (Instant Chromosomal Error in Meiosis): Nondisjunction produces extra chromosome sets (3n, 4n) — Autopolyploidy (within 1 species) or Allopolyploidy (hybrid of 2 species). A tetraploid (4n) plant is instantly reproductively isolated from its diploid (2n) parent because 4n × 2n → sterile 3n triploid offspring!
    • 2. Habitat / Host Shift Differentiation: Apple maggot flies (Rhagoletis pomonella) shifting from native hawthorn trees to introduced apple trees.
    • 3. Sexual Selection: Female Lake Victoria cichlid fish selecting males of specific colors under different light wavelengths.
Polyploidy is an extremely common mechanism of sympatric speciation in PLANTS (e.g., wheat, cotton, strawberries), NOT most animals!
4. Pace of Evolution & Macroevolutionary Patterns Gradualism vs. Punctuated
  • Gradualism vs. Punctuated Equilibrium (Eldredge & Gould):
    • Phyletic Gradualism: Evolution proceeds slowly and continuously as small adaptive changes accumulate over millions of years.
    • Punctuated Equilibrium: Species experience long geological periods of morphological stasis (equilibrium) punctuated by rapid bursts of speciation when environments change sharply.
  • Divergent Evolution & Adaptive Radiation:
    • Divergent Evolution: Closely related populations from a common ancestor accumulate differences to adapt to different habitats.
    • Adaptive Radiation: Rapid evolution of many diverse species from a single ancestral species when novel ecological niches open up (e.g., Darwin's Galápagos finches, Hawaiian silverswords, Cenozoic mammals after dinosaur extinction).
  • Convergent Evolution: Unrelated species evolve similar analogous traits under similar environmental pressures (does not indicate close ancestry!).

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 1, 2 & 3 (Artificial Selection, Hardy-Weinberg Mathematical Modeling & BLAST Bioinformatics): Comparing allele frequency shifts across generations under directional selection vs. small-population genetic drift simulations, and constructing maximum-parsimony cladograms from BLAST percentage sequence identity matrices (Cytochrome c / β-globin). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Golden Rule: Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE): Allele frequencies p + q = 1; Genotype frequencies p² (homozygous dominant) + 2pq (heterozygous carriers) + q² (homozygous recessive) = 1. Golden Calculation Sequence: ALWAYS begin with the recessive phenotype frequency (q²), take √(q²) to find q, compute p = 1 − q, and then calculate 2pq (heterozygote carrier frequency). Never take the square root of the dominant phenotype frequency (p² + 2pq)! Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Evolutionary & Biomedical Disruption Case Study: Incomplete antibiotic regimens or agricultural antibiotic overuse exert strong directional selection on bacterial populations (e.g., MRSA or MDR-Tuberculosis): susceptible cells die while rare, pre-existing resistant mutants (arising via random mutation or R-plasmid conjugation) survive and reproduce. In conservation biology, severe population bottlenecks (e.g., Northern Elephant Seals, African Cheetahs) drastically erode genetic diversity via genetic drift, increasing homozygosity for deleterious recessive alleles and vulnerability to novel pathogens.
