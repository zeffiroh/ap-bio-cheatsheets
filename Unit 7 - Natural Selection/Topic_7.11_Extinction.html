1. Genetic Diversity & Population Resilience Biological Insurance
  • Genetic Diversity = Variety of Alleles Within a Population: Determines how well a population can respond to biotic or abiotic environmental shifts.
  • High Genetic Diversity = Greater Resilience:
    • More allelic variation increases the probability that at least some individuals express phenotypes capable of surviving a novel pathogen, toxin, or climate shift.
    • Those survivors reproduce and allow the species to persist rather than go extinct.
  • Low Genetic Diversity = High Extinction Risk:
    • Genetically uniform populations share the same vulnerabilities — a single novel disease or environmental change can wipe out the entire population.
    • Genetic drift reduces diversity in small populations, whereas gene flow and balancing selection (heterozygote advantage) help maintain allele diversity.
Genetic diversity = biological insurance against environmental change.
Large population size ≠ high genetic diversity! A population can have millions of individuals today (e.g., Northern elephant seals or monoculture crops) yet possess dangerously low genetic diversity due to a past bottleneck or clonal propagation!
High-Yield AP Case Studies of Low Genetic Diversity Vulnerability:
  • Wild Cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus): Survived two severe historical bottlenecks → 99% genetically identical (can accept skin grafts from unrelated cheetahs!); highly vulnerable to feline coronavirus (FIP) and poor sperm quality.
  • Tasmanian Devils: Low MHC immune gene diversity allowed contagious Devil Facial Tumor Disease (DFTD) to spread without immune rejection.
  • Cavendish Bananas & Irish Potato Famine (1840s): Clonal asexual monocultures devastated by Panama disease fungus (TR4) and Phytophthora infestans water mold.
  • California Condors & Prairie Chickens: Severe bottlenecks caused inbreeding depression and reduced egg hatchability until rescued by genetic translocation (gene flow).
2. Context-Dependent Allele Fitness & Balancing Selection Changing Pressures
  • Allele Fitness Depends on Environmental Context: An allele that is deleterious or neutral in one environment can become strongly advantageous if the environment changes.
  • Balancing Selection: Preserves multiple alleles at higher frequencies in a gene pool via:
    • Heterozygote Advantage: E.g., Sickle-cell allele (HbS) confers malaria resistance in HbA/HbS heterozygotes in sub-Saharan Africa, but is disadvantageous where malaria is absent.
    • Frequency-Dependent Selection: Fitness of a phenotype depends on how common it is (e.g., scale-eating cichlid fish mouth asymmetry).
3. Extinction: Background vs. Mass Extinctions CED EVO-3.G – 3.J
  • Why Extinction Occurs: Extinction happens when environmental conditions change faster than a species can adapt (insufficient genetic variation to match selective pressures). Over 99% of all species that ever lived are now extinct!
  • Background Extinction Rate: Slow, steady baseline rate of extinction resulting from normal ecological interactions (competition, predation, local habitat shifts).
  • 5 Historical Mass Extinctions ("The Big Five"): Global, rapid catastrophic events where 50%–96% of all species were wiped out across Earth in a geologically short period:
    • Permian-Triassic ("Great Dying," ~252 mya): Massive Siberian volcanic eruptions, global warming, and ocean anoxia/acidification killed ~96% of marine species and 70% of terrestrial vertebrates.
    • Cretaceous-Paleogene (K–Pg / K–T, ~66 mya): Chicxulub asteroid impact (iridium layer in clay) + Deccan Traps volcanism blocked sunlight, collapsing photosynthetic food webs and wiping out non-avian dinosaurs (~75% of all species).
  • 6th Mass Extinction (Anthropocene / Holocene): Currently driven by human activities — habitat destruction/fragmentation, climate change, ocean acidification, pollution, overexploitation, and invasive species — at 100 to 1,000× the background rate.
4. Ecological Consequence: Vacant Niches & Adaptive Radiation EVO-3.I
  • Mass Extinctions Open Ecological Niches: When dominant competitors/predators are eliminated, surviving lineages experience ecological release and rapidly diversify via adaptive radiation to fill the newly vacant niches (though recovery takes 5–10+ million years).

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 1, 2 & 3 (Artificial Selection, Hardy-Weinberg Mathematical Modeling & BLAST Bioinformatics): Comparing allele frequency shifts across generations under directional selection vs. small-population genetic drift simulations, and constructing maximum-parsimony cladograms from BLAST percentage sequence identity matrices (Cytochrome c / β-globin). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Golden Rule: Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE): Allele frequencies p + q = 1; Genotype frequencies p² (homozygous dominant) + 2pq (heterozygous carriers) + q² (homozygous recessive) = 1. Golden Calculation Sequence: ALWAYS begin with the recessive phenotype frequency (q²), take √(q²) to find q, compute p = 1 − q, and then calculate 2pq (heterozygote carrier frequency). Never take the square root of the dominant phenotype frequency (p² + 2pq)! Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Evolutionary & Biomedical Disruption Case Study: Incomplete antibiotic regimens or agricultural antibiotic overuse exert strong directional selection on bacterial populations (e.g., MRSA or MDR-Tuberculosis): susceptible cells die while rare, pre-existing resistant mutants (arising via random mutation or R-plasmid conjugation) survive and reproduce. In conservation biology, severe population bottlenecks (e.g., Northern Elephant Seals, African Cheetahs) drastically erode genetic diversity via genetic drift, increasing homozygosity for deleterious recessive alleles and vulnerability to novel pathogens.
