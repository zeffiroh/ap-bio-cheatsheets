Phenotypic Plasticity: Same Genotype, Different Phenotype Gene Expression Shift
  • Definition: Phenotypic plasticity occurs when individuals with the same genotype exhibit different phenotypes when exposed to different environmental conditions.
  • Molecular Mechanism: Environmental signals alter gene expression (transcription factor activity, epigenetic chromatin modification) or protein/enzyme activity — does NOT alter the underlying DNA nucleotide sequence!
  • Norm of Reaction: A graph plotting the range of phenotypes produced by a single genotype across a continuous environmental gradient (e.g., plant height across elevation or temperature).
Genotype Stays Constant: Only the level of transcription/translation or enzyme functionality changes in response to the environment.
Analogy: Think of the genotype as the same “recipe” producing a different “dish” depending on oven conditions — or the environment acting as a “dimmer switch” for genes!
Plasticity ≠ Mutation: Never say an environmental factor “mutated the gene to fit the environment” in phenotypic plasticity — no DNA sequence change occurs!

Soil pH & Aluminum (Al³⁺) Hydrangea Flower Color Same genotype produces blue flowers in acidic soil (pH < 5.5) (free Al³⁺ ions are soluble and absorbed, complexing with anthocyanin pigment) vs. pink flowers in alkaline/neutral soil (Al³⁺ precipitates out and is unavailable).

Evolutionary Advantages & Limitations of Plasticity Fitness & Trade-Offs
  • Evolutionary Advantages of Phenotypic Plasticity:
    • Rapid Adaptability Within One Lifetime: Allows an individual organism to respond to fluctuating environmental conditions immediately without waiting for slow generational changes in allele frequencies.
    • Enhanced Survival & Fitness: Organisms achieve higher reproductive success across heterogeneous or unpredictable habitats.
    • Resource & Energy Efficiency: Allocates metabolic energy to inducible traits (e.g., chemical defenses or protective structures) only when needed, avoiding the cost of maintaining permanent structures.
  • Limitations & Evolutionary Costs of Plasticity:
    • Metabolic Energy Cost: Maintaining sensory receptors and regulatory machinery for flexible gene expression requires more energy than a genetically fixed phenotype.
    • Physiological Trade-Offs: Enhancing one trait often comes at the expense of another (e.g., shade plants elongating stems rapidly toward light develop weaker root systems and thinner stems).
    • Environmental Mismatch Risk: If environmental cues are unreliable or change faster than the plastic response time, the induced phenotype may become maladaptive.
Desert Plants & Water Stress: Many desert shrubs shed leaves or develop smaller, thicker, waxier leaves during drought seasons to minimize stomatal transpiration water loss, then grow larger photosynthetic leaves after seasonal rains.
Plasticity Has Costs & Limits: Do not assume phenotypic plasticity is always beneficial or limitless — extreme environments beyond a species' physiological tolerance still cause death, and plastic traits are constrained by energetic trade-offs.

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigation 7 Part 2 (Sordaria fimicola Meiotic Crossing Over) & Drosophila/Fast Plants Crosses: Counting 4:4 non-recombinant vs. 2:2:2:2 / 2:4:2 recombinant asci in Sordaria perithecia to determine gene-to-centromere map distance (% Crossover = [Recombinant asci / Total asci] × 100; Map distance in cM = % Crossover / 2 because only 2 of 4 chromatids in a tetrad cross over). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Statistical Testing: Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit: χ² = Σ [(O − E)² / E], with Degrees of Freedom df = (number of phenotypic classes − 1). At α = 0.05: if χ² > critical value, REJECT H₀ (e.g., genes are autosomal linked rather than assorting independently 9:3:3:1); if χ² ≤ critical value, FAIL TO REJECT H₀. Recombination Frequency: RF = (Total Recombinants / Total Offspring) × 100% = Map Units (cM). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Clinical & Chromosomal Disruption Case Study: Meiotic Nondisjunction (failure of homologous chromosomes to separate in Anaphase I, or sister chromatids to separate in Anaphase II) yields aneuploid gametes (n + 1 or n − 1). Fertilization produces Trisomy 21 (Down Syndrome, 47, +21), Klinefelter Syndrome (47, XXY), or monosomic Turner Syndrome (45, X0). Mitochondrial myopathies (e.g., Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy) exhibit strict maternal inheritance because the ovum contributes virtually all zygotic cytoplasm and mitochondria.
