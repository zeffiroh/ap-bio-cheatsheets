1. Direct Cell-to-Cell Contact (Juxtacrine & Junctions) Physical Touch
  • Overview: Cells communicate by physical contact via cytoplasmic junctions or direct membrane-bound surface protein binding. Essential for tissue coordination, embryonic development, and immune recognition.
  • Gap Junctions (Animal Cells Only):
    • Protein channels (connexons) that directly connect the cytoplasm of adjacent animal cells.
    • Allow rapid passage of ions (Ca²⁺, Na⁺) and small water-soluble signaling molecules (e.g., cAMP, IP₃) without crossing the plasma membrane.
  • Plasmodesmata (Plant Cells Only):
    • Membrane-lined channels that tunnel directly through rigid cellulose plant cell walls, creating a continuous cytoplasmic network (the symplast).
    • Allow movement of water, ions, small signaling molecules, and some regulatory proteins/RNAs between adjacent plant cells.
  • Direct Receptor–Ligand Contact (Juxtacrine Signaling):
    • A membrane-bound ligand protein on one cell's surface binds directly to a complementary receptor protein on an adjacent cell.
  • Gap Junctions: Cardiac (heart) muscle cells pass electrical ion currents through gap junctions for synchronized heart contraction.
  • Direct Surface Contact: Helper & Cytotoxic T cells (TCR + CD4/CD8) directly bind antigen fragments displayed on MHC proteins of Antigen-Presenting Cells (APCs) to trigger adaptive immunity!

2. Short-Distance (Local) Signaling Paracrine, Synaptic & Autocrine
  • Overview: Cells secrete chemical local regulators into the extracellular fluid that diffuse over short distances to affect nearby cells; responses are typically rapid and short-lived.
  • Paracrine Signaling (Neighboring Cells):
    • Signaling cell releases chemical messengers that diffuse locally through interstitial fluid to stimulate adjacent target cells in the same tissue.
    • Examples: Growth factors (PDGF, EGF) stimulating nearby cells to divide during wound healing; histamine during local inflammation; nitric oxide (NO) vasodilation.
  • Synaptic Signaling (Nervous System):
    • An electrical action potential reaches a neuron's axon terminal, triggering exocytosis of neurotransmitters across a tiny gap (synaptic cleft) to bind receptors on the postsynaptic target cell (neuron or muscle cell).
    • Example: Acetylcholine (ACh) released at the neuromuscular junction triggers rapid skeletal muscle contraction.
  • Autocrine Signaling (Self-Signaling):
    • A cell secretes a ligand that binds to receptors on its OWN plasma membrane to reinforce or amplify a response.
    • Examples: Helper T cells secreting Interleukin-2 (IL-2) to stimulate their own clonal proliferation; cancer cells overproducing growth factors to drive their own unchecked division.
  • Quorum Sensing in Bacteria (Prokaryotic Local Signaling):
    • Unicellular bacteria secrete small signaling molecules called autoinducers (e.g., AHL). As bacterial population density rises, autoinducer concentration crosses a threshold, binding receptors to coordinate group gene expression — such as forming protective biofilms, producing bioluminescence (Vibrio fischeri), or releasing virulence toxins.
Autocrine vs. Paracrine Trap: Autocrine signaling does NOT target neighboring cells first — the secreted ligand binds directly to receptors on the exact same cell that released it!
3. Long-Distance (Endocrine) Signaling & Specificity Hormones & Target Cells
  • Endocrine Signaling: Specialized endocrine cells secrete hormones that travel long distances through the body to reach distant target organs:
    • In Animals: Hormones travel through the circulatory system (bloodstream).
    • In Plants: Plant hormones (auxin, gibberellins, abscisic acid) travel through vascular tissue (xylem/phloem) or as a gas through air spaces (ethylene).
  • Why Only Specific Cells Respond: Although a hormone in the bloodstream contacts virtually every cell in the body, ONLY target cells that express the specific, complementary receptor protein can bind the hormone and respond!

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigation 7 (Mitosis in Onion Root Tips & Lectin Signaling): Root tip squash stained with aceto-orcein/toluidine blue to compare mitotic index (% dividing cells = [cells in PMAT / total cells counted] × 100) between control meristem and phytohemagglutinin (lectin) or caffeine-treated roots. Chi-square (χ²) tests whether observed mitotic vs. interphase counts differ significantly from control. Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative & Graph Analysis: Mitotic Index = (N_mitosis / N_total) × 100. DNA Content (C) & Ploidy (n) tracking in diploid (2n) somatic cells: G₁ = 2n, 2C (unreplicated chromosomes); S phase = DNA synthesis (2n, 2C → 4C); G₂/Prophase/Metaphase = 2n, 4C (sister chromatids joined); Anaphase = transient 4n, 4C (chromatids separate into individual chromosomes); Post-Cytokinesis = 2n, 2C. Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Clinical & Signaling Disruption Case Study: Vibrio cholerae secretes Cholera Toxin, which enzymatically modifies the G_α subunit of intestinal GPCRs so it cannot hydrolyze GTP → GDP. Locked in its active GTP-bound state, G_α continuously activates adenylyl cyclase, causing runaway cAMP accumulation, constitutive PKA activation, CFTR Cl⁻/H₂O efflux, and life-threatening secretory diarrhea. In cell cycle control, loss-of-function mutations in both alleles of TP53 ('guardian of the genome') prevent p21-mediated G₁/S arrest and apoptosis of DNA-damaged cells, driving oncogenic proliferation.
