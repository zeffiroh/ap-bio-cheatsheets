Concentration & Electrochemical Gradients Potential Energy
  • Concentration Gradient: Spatial difference in solute concentration across a membrane:
    • Down the Gradient ([High] → [Low]): Spontaneous (ΔG < 0, increases entropy) → Passive Transport (NO ATP required); leads toward dynamic equilibrium (net movement = 0, though molecules keep moving equally in both directions!).
    • Against the Gradient ([Low] → [High]): Non-spontaneous (ΔG > 0) → Active Transport (Requires metabolic energy / ATP).
  • 3 Types of Driving Gradients Across Membranes:
    • 1. Chemical Gradient: Difference in solute concentration across the membrane.
    • 2. Electrical Gradient (Membrane Potential): Difference in electrical charge (voltage) across the membrane (cytoplasm is typically negative, −70 mV, relative to extracellular fluid).
    • 3. Electrochemical Gradient: Combined force of both the chemical concentration gradient AND electrical charge gradient acting on a charged ion (Na⁺, K⁺, H⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻).
A concentration or electrochemical gradient = stored potential energy (like water behind a dam) that cells can harvest to drive work or ATP synthesis!
Whenever an AP Biology question asks what drives the movement of charged ions (Na⁺, K⁺, H⁺, Ca²⁺) across a membrane, always write "electrochemical gradient" (not just concentration gradient)!

Active Transport: Moving Uphill Against the Gradient Requires ATP
  • Core Requirements: Moves specific solutes against their electrochemical gradient ([Low] → [High]); always requires integral carrier/pump proteins AND metabolic energy (ATP).
  • Primary Active Transport (Direct ATP Coupling):
    • Hydrolysis of ATP → ADP + Pᵢ directly phosphorylates the transport pump, triggering a conformational shape change that translocates ions uphill.
    • Classic Example — Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase Pump: Pumps 3 Na⁺ OUT of the cell and 2 K⁺ INTO the cell per 1 ATP hydrolyzed, maintaining a negative resting potential inside.
Bulk Transport: Endocytosis & Exocytosis Macromolecules & Vesicles
  • Large macromolecules (proteins, polysaccharides, polynucleotides) and whole cells/particles cannot pass through transport proteins → cross via ATP-requiring membrane vesicle remodeling (enabled by phospholipid bilayer fluidity):
  • 1. Endocytosis (Bulk Uptake INTO Cell): Plasma membrane invaginates (folds inward) to form a pocket that pinches off as an intracellular vesicle:
    • Phagocytosis ("Cell Eating"): Pseudopodia engulf large solid particles, cell debris, or whole bacteria into a food vacuole (phagosome), which fuses with a lysosome for digestion (e.g., Amoeba feeding; human macrophages engulfing pathogens).
    • Pinocytosis ("Cell Drinking"): Non-specific uptake of tiny droplets of extracellular fluid and dissolved solutes into small pinocytotic vesicles.
    • Receptor-Mediated Endocytosis (Highly Specific): Extracellular ligands bind specific membrane receptor proteins clustered in clathrin-coated pits → folds inward to form a coated vesicle (e.g., human liver cells taking up cholesterol-carrying LDL particles; defective in familial hypercholesterolemia!).
  • 2. Exocytosis (Bulk Secretion OUT OF Cell):
    • Intracellular transport/secretory vesicles (budded from the trans-Golgi) migrate along microtubule tracks to the plasma membrane.
    • Vesicle lipid bilayer fuses with the plasma membrane, expelling its contents outside while adding new phospholipids & proteins to expand the plasma membrane!
    • Key Biological Roles: Secretion of peptide hormones (insulin from pancreatic β-cells), neurotransmitters (acetylcholine/dopamine at synaptic clefts), digestive enzymes, antibodies, plant cell wall polysaccharides, and waste expulsion.
Why Membrane Transport Matters for Life Cellular Homeostasis
  • Homeostasis & Bioenergetics: Cells cannot exist at chemical equilibrium with their surroundings (equilibrium = death!). Coordinated transport maintains:
    • Resting & Action Potentials across neuron and muscle membranes.
    • Proton Gradients (H⁺) for oxidative phosphorylation and photophosphorylation.
    • Selective Nutrient Import (glucose, amino acids) & Metabolic Waste Export (CO₂, urea, lactate).
    • Intercellular Communication via exocytosis of neurotransmitters and hormones.
Cells constantly balance two complementary strategies:
  • Passive Transport (Simple + Facilitated): Energy-efficient (0 ATP), rapid movement down existing gradients.
  • Active + Bulk Transport: Energy-investing (uses ATP), tightly regulated control against gradients or for large cargo.

IV: Extracellular sucrose molarity (M); DV: % change in mass; Isotonic point: x-intercept where % ΔMass = 0% (no net water movement). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Graph Rules: Total Water Potential: Ψ = Ψ_p + Ψ_s. Solute Potential: Ψ_s = −iCRT (where i = ionization constant [1 for sucrose/glucose, 2 for NaCl], C = molarity in mol/L, R = 0.0831 L·bar/mol·K, T = K = °C + 273). Surface-Area-to-Volume Ratio (SA:V): Cube SA = 6s², V = s³ (SA:V = 6/s). Larger SA:V accelerates diffusion per unit volume. Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).

Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Clinical & Physiological Disruption Case Study: Cystic Fibrosis results from a deletion (ΔF508) in the CFTR chloride channel protein, preventing misfolded channels from reaching the apical plasma membrane. Impaired Cl⁻ efflux prevents osmotic water movement into mucus, producing thick, dehydrated mucus in lungs and pancreatic ducts.
