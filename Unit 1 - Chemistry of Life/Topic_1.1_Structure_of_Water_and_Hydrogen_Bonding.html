Molecular Polarity & Hydrogen Bonding Bent V-Shape (104.5°)
  • Intramolecular Polar Covalent Bonds: Within a single H₂O molecule, oxygen is highly electronegative (3.44) compared to hydrogen (2.20), pulling shared valence electrons closer to the O nucleus.
  • Permanent Electric Dipoles: Unequal electron sharing + bent geometry (2 lone pairs on O) creates:
    • Partial Negative Charge (2δ⁻) at the oxygen apex.
    • Partial Positive Charges (δ⁺) at the two hydrogen atoms.
  • Intermolecular Hydrogen Bonds: Electrostatic attraction between the δ⁺ H of one water molecule and the δ⁻ lone pair on O (or N/F) of an adjacent molecule.
    • Individually weak (~20 kJ/mol, ~1/20th of a covalent bond) and transient, but collectively strong across billions of molecules (up to 4 H-bonds per H₂O).
Never confuse bond types: Heating/boiling water breaks intermolecular hydrogen bonds BETWEEN H₂O molecules — it does NOT break the intramolecular polar covalent O–H bonds within a water molecule!
Cohesion, Adhesion & Surface Tension Capillary Action
  • Cohesion (Water–Water Attraction): Hydrogen bonding holds water molecules together as a continuous column under tension.
  • Adhesion (Water–Polar Surface Attraction): Water forms H-bonds with polar or charged surfaces (e.g., cellulose microfibrils in xylem cell walls or SiO₂ in glass).
  • Transpiration-Cohesion-Tension Mechanism:
    • Evaporation of water from leaf stomata generates negative water potential (tension) → cohesion pulls the unbroken water column upward from roots to shoots, while adhesion to hydrophilic xylem walls resists gravity.
  • High Surface Tension: Water molecules at the air–water interface H-bond strongly to neighbors below and beside them, creating an elastic "skin" at the surface.
Water striders (Gerris) walk on ponds without sinking due to surface tension; vascular plants transport water >100 m up giant redwoods via cohesion + adhesion in xylem vessels without spending ATP.
Cohesion = Co-worker (water sticks to water).
Adhesion = Adhesive tape (water sticks to other polar surfaces).
Thermal Moderation & Density Anomaly of Ice Homeostasis
  • High Specific Heat Capacity (4.184 J/g·°C):
    • Absorbed thermal energy must first break hydrogen bonds before increasing the kinetic energy (temperature) of water molecules.
    • When cooling, forming H-bonds releases heat → buffers coastal climates and stabilizes cytosolic/ocean temperatures for enzyme activity.
  • High Heat of Vaporization & Evaporative Cooling:
    • Takes ~2,260 J to vaporize 1 g of liquid water because all H-bonds must be broken for a molecule to escape as gas.
    • As the highest-kinetic-energy ("hottest") H₂O molecules evaporate, the average kinetic energy of remaining liquid drops → sweating in mammals & transpiration in leaves prevent overheating.
  • Expansion Upon Freezing (Ice Floats):
    • In liquid water (highest density at 4°C), H-bonds constantly break and reform, allowing close packing.
    • At 0°C, each H₂O locks into a rigid, open tetrahedral crystalline lattice stabilized by 4 H-bonds → ~9% less dense than liquid water.
    • Biological Significance: Floating surface ice insulates liquid water below, preventing lakes and oceans from freezing solid from the bottom up.
Water as the Universal Biological Solvent Hydration Shells
  • Hydration Shells Around Ions: When an ionic compound (e.g., NaCl) dissolves in water, water's polar dipoles dissociate the crystal lattice:
    • Partially negative oxygen (δ⁻) faces positively charged cations (Na⁺).
    • Partially positive hydrogens (δ⁺) face negatively charged anions (Cl⁻).
  • Hydrophilic ("Water-Loving"): Ions and polar molecules (sugars with -OH, amino acids, proteins with charged surface R-groups) form H-bonds or ion-dipole interactions with water and dissolve readily.
  • Hydrophobic ("Water-Fearing"): Nonpolar molecules rich in symmetric C–H and C–C bonds (fats, oils, steroid rings, hydrocarbon tails) cannot H-bond with water → water forms cage-like H-bonds around them, driving hydrophobic exclusion (membrane bilayer assembly & interior protein folding).

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Lab Context: Macromolecule Indicator Assays (Benedict's for reducing sugars, Lugol's Iodine for starch, Biuret for peptide bonds) & Buffer Titration Curves. Independent Variable (IV): pH or temperature treatment; Dependent Variable (DV): absorbance/colorimetric change or structural stability; Negative Control: deionized H₂O. Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative & Graph Analysis: Stoichiometric monomer-to-polymer condensation: for n monomers joined in a linear chain, (n − 1) covalent bonds form and (n − 1) H₂O molecules are released. pH scale is logarithmic: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; each 1-unit decrease in pH represents a 10-fold increase in [H⁺]. Caloric density: Lipids (~9 kcal/g) vs. Carbohydrates/Proteins (~4 kcal/g). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Clinical & Molecular Disruption Case Study: Missense mutation in the β-globin gene (Glu6Val in Sickle-Cell Disease) replaces a polar/charged glutamic acid R-group with a nonpolar hydrophobic valine on the protein exterior, driving abnormal quaternary hydrophobic aggregation into rigid fibers under deoxygenated conditions. Similarly, extreme fever (>40°C) or acidosis (pH < 7.0) disrupts weak hydrogen and ionic bonds in tertiary (3°) protein structure while leaving primary (1°) peptide bonds intact.
