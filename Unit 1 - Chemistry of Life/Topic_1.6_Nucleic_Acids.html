  • 3 Core Components of Every Nucleotide:
    • 5-Carbon Pentose Sugar:
      • Deoxyribose (in DNA): Has a hydrogen (–H) on the 2′ carbon → chemically more stable, resists alkaline hydrolysis.
      • Ribose (in RNA): Has a reactive hydroxyl (–OH) on the 2′ carbon → less stable, prone to self-cleavage.
    • Phosphate Group (–PO₄²⁻): Attached to the 5′ carbon of the sugar; imparts a negative charge and acidity.
    • Nitrogenous Base (Attached to 1′ carbon):
      • Purines (2 fused C–N rings): Adenine (A) and Guanine (G) — “Pure As Gold = 2 rings.”
      • Pyrimidines (1 single C–N ring): Cytosine (C), Thymine (T, in DNA only), and Uracil (U, in RNA only) — “CUT the Py = 1 ring.”
  • Roles Beyond Genetic Storage:
    • ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate): Ribonucleotide with 3 phosphate groups used for cellular energy transfer.
    • Signaling & Coenzymes: cAMP (second messenger), NAD⁺ / FAD (electron carriers), and GTP.
Nucleotide = 3-part unit (5C Pentose Sugar + 5′ Phosphate Group + 1′ Nitrogenous Base).
Sugar-Phosphate Backbone & 5′ → 3′ Directionality Phosphodiester Bonds
  • Phosphodiester Linkage (Covalent): Dehydration synthesis links the 5′ phosphate group of an incoming nucleotide to the 3′ hydroxyl (–OH) group of the last nucleotide in the chain.
  • Linear Strand Polarity (Directionality):
    • 5′ End: Terminates with a free phosphate group (–PO₄²⁻) on the 5′ carbon.
    • 3′ End: Terminates with a free hydroxyl group (–OH) on the 3′ carbon.
  • Rule of Nucleic Acid Synthesis (DNA Pol & RNA Pol):
    • Strands ALWAYS grow in the 5′ → 3′ direction!
    • Incoming nucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs/NTPs) are always added to the free 3′-OH end (cleaving pyrophosphate PPᵢ provides the energy for polymerization).
Never mix up backbone vs. rung bonds:
• Sugar-phosphate backbone = joined by strong covalent phosphodiester bonds.
• Complementary base pairs = joined by weak hydrogen bonds (so helicase can unzip strands during replication/transcription without breaking the genetic code!).
Backbone = ladder rails (structural stability); Bases = information steps (sequence of bases = genetic code).
Antiparallel Double Helix & Chargaff’s Rules A=T (2) • G≡C (3)
  • Antiparallel Double Helix: Two polynucleotide strands coil around a central axis running in opposite directions (5′ → 3′ paired alongside 3′ → 5′).
  • Complementary Base Pairing (Purine always pairs with Pyrimidine → uniform ~2 nm diameter):
    • DNA Base Pairing: A–T forms 2 hydrogen bonds; G–C forms 3 hydrogen bonds.
    • RNA Base Pairing: A–U (2 H-bonds) and G–C (3 H-bonds).
  • Why Base Pairing Matters: Enables accurate template-based DNA replication, transcription (DNA → mRNA), and translation (codon–anticodon pairing).
  • Chargaff’s Rules (Applies to Double-Stranded DNA):
    • In any double-stranded DNA sample: %A = %T and %G = %C (total Purines A+G = 50% = total Pyrimidines C+T = 50%).
    • Example Calculation: If dsDNA is 18% Adenine → 18% Thymine (36% A+T total) → remaining 64% is G+C → 32% Guanine & 32% Cytosine.
    • Single-stranded RNA does NOT obey Chargaff's rules (unless folded into base-paired hairpins).
Thermal Stability & G–C Content: Because C–G pairs form 3 H-bonds vs. 2 H-bonds in A–T pairs, DNA regions with a higher GC percentage are harder to denature (require a higher melting temperature, Tₘ, to separate strands).
For full FRQ credit on DNA structure questions, always explicitly state BOTH terms: 1) Antiparallel strands (5′→3′ / 3′→5′) AND 2) Complementary hydrogen-bonded base pairing (A–T, G–C).
DNA vs. RNA Comparison Storage vs. Expression
Feature DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) RNA (Ribonucleic Acid)
Pentose Sugar Deoxyribose (2′ –H, lacks O) Ribose (2′ –OH hydroxyl group)
Nitrogenous Bases A, G, C, Thymine (T) A, G, C, Uracil (U)
Strandedness Double-stranded (antiparallel helix) Single-stranded (can fold into 3D shapes)
Chemical Stability More stable (2′-H + double helix) Less stable (2′-OH susceptible to hydrolysis)
Biological Function Long-term hereditary storage Short-term gene expression / catalysis (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, ribozymes)
DNA = permanent hard drive (stable archive in the nucleus); RNA = temporary working file (disposable photocopy used for protein synthesis).
AP CED Scope Note: Memorizing the exact atomic ring structures of individual purine/pyrimidine bases is not required on the AP Exam — know which bases are purines (A, G — 2 rings) vs. pyrimidines (C, T, U — 1 ring) and their H-bond counts!

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Lab Context: Macromolecule Indicator Assays (Benedict's for reducing sugars, Lugol's Iodine for starch, Biuret for peptide bonds) & Buffer Titration Curves. Independent Variable (IV): pH or temperature treatment; Dependent Variable (DV): absorbance/colorimetric change or structural stability; Negative Control: deionized H₂O. Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative & Graph Analysis: Stoichiometric monomer-to-polymer condensation: for n monomers joined in a linear chain, (n − 1) covalent bonds form and (n − 1) H₂O molecules are released. pH scale is logarithmic: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; each 1-unit decrease in pH represents a 10-fold increase in [H⁺]. Caloric density: Lipids (~9 kcal/g) vs. Carbohydrates/Proteins (~4 kcal/g). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Clinical & Molecular Disruption Case Study: Missense mutation in the β-globin gene (Glu6Val in Sickle-Cell Disease) replaces a polar/charged glutamic acid R-group with a nonpolar hydrophobic valine on the protein exterior, driving abnormal quaternary hydrophobic aggregation into rigid fibers under deoxygenated conditions. Similarly, extreme fever (>40°C) or acidosis (pH < 7.0) disrupts weak hydrogen and ionic bonds in tertiary (3°) protein structure while leaving primary (1°) peptide bonds intact.
