Enzymes as Biological Catalysts Speed Up Reactions
  • Biological Catalysts: Enzymes are globular proteins (or catalytic RNAs/ribozymes) that accelerate chemical reactions without being altered or consumed.
  • Function by Lowering Activation Energy (Eₐ): Reduce the initial energy barrier needed to break existing bonds and reach the transition state, allowing metabolic reactions to occur fast enough to sustain life at physiological temperatures.
  • Reusability & High Turnover: Because enzymes are not consumed in the reaction, a single enzyme molecule can be reused repeatedly (catalyzing 10³–10⁷ reactions per second).
  • Do Not Alter ΔG: Enzymes never change the free energy of reactants or products (ΔG remains identical) and cannot make an endergonic reaction exergonic.
Enzyme = lowers the hill (activation energy Eₐ) for the reaction so more reactant molecules have sufficient kinetic energy to react per unit time.
Do NOT say enzymes "add energy" or "provide heat" to a reaction → they lower the activation energy barrier (Eₐ) while keeping overall free energy change (ΔG) completely unchanged!
Transition State Stabilization & Energy Profile Lowering Eₐ vs ΔG
  • Transition State (‡): All chemical reactions pass through an unstable, high-energy intermediate state where bonds are contorted and on the verge of breaking/forming.
  • How Active Sites Lower Eₐ:
    • Orienting Substrates: Brings two reactants together in the exact 3D geometric orientation needed to react.
    • Straining Substrate Bonds: Physically stretches/bends critical covalent bonds toward their transition-state conformation.
    • Microenvironment & Direct Catalysis: Provides acidic/basic R-groups or temporary covalent intermediates that stabilize charges in the transition state.
Mechanism of Enzyme Action (4 Core Steps) Catalytic Cycle
  • Step 1 — Binding: Substrate enters and binds to the enzyme's active site via weak noncovalent bonds (H-bonds, ionic, hydrophobic), forming the transient enzyme-substrate (ES) complex.
  • Step 2 — Catalysis: Active site R-groups stabilize the transition state → activation energy (Eₐ) is lowered.
  • Step 3 — Conversion: Substrate covalent bonds are broken or formed, converting substrate → product(s) inside the ES complex.
  • Step 4 — Release: Products have lower affinity for the active site and are released; the unchanged enzyme is immediately free to bind another substrate molecule.
Catalytic Reaction Sequence:
E + S ⇌ ES Complex → E + Products (P)
Active Site & Structural Basis of Specificity Shape + Charge
  • Active Site: A specialized 3D pocket or groove on the enzyme's surface formed by specific amino acid R-group residues brought together by tertiary (3°) protein folding.
  • Dual Requirement for Binding: A substrate must possess both:
    • Complementary 3D Shape (steric fit into the active site pocket).
    • Complementary Charge & Polarity (acidic/negatively charged active site R-groups attract basic/positively charged substrate regions; nonpolar pockets bind hydrophobic substrates).
  • Weak Stabilizing Interactions in ES Complex:
    • Hydrogen bonds between polar side chains and substrate functional groups.
    • Ionic interactions (salt bridges) between charged R-groups (-COO⁻, -NH₃⁺) and substrate ions.
    • Hydrophobic / van der Waals interactions excluding water from the catalytic pocket.
Active site = Lock | Substrate = Key
Changes in 3D protein structure → altered active site → loss of function!
Lock-and-Key vs. Induced Fit Models Dynamic Conformation
Model Active Site Behavior Biological Accuracy
Lock-and-Key Active site is completely rigid; pre-shaped as an exact static mold for the substrate. Outdated; explains specificity but fails to explain transition-state stabilization.
Induced Fit (Accepted) Active site is flexible; substrate binding induces a conformational change that wraps tightly around the substrate. AP Standard: Explains how enzymes mold around substrates to strain bonds and catalyze reactions.
Why Specificity Matters & Enzyme Regulators Metabolic Control
  • Biological Importance of Specificity:
    • Ensures only the correct metabolic reactions occur at each step of a pathway.
    • Prevents wasteful or harmful side reactions in the crowded cytosol.
    • Allows independent, precise regulation of metabolic pathways.
  • Inhibitors & Activators Overview:
    • Inhibitors (↓ Activity): Competitive inhibitors bind directly to the active site (compete with substrate); Noncompetitive inhibitors bind elsewhere (allosteric site) and warp the active site's shape.
    • Activators (↑ Efficiency): Cofactors (inorganic ions like Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺), coenzymes (organic vitamins like NAD⁺), or allosteric activators stabilize the active conformation.
Salivary Amylase specifically hydrolyzes α-1,4 glycosidic bonds in starch only — it cannot break down proteins or even cellulose (β-1,4 bonds) due to active-site stereospecificity!
Exam Trap: Remember that competitive inhibition CAN be overcome by increasing substrate concentration ([S]) so substrate outcompetes the inhibitor for the active site, whereas noncompetitive inhibition cannot!

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 5, 6 & 13 (Enzyme Catalysis, Photosynthesis Leaf Disks & Respirometry): Enzyme assay (catalase/peroxidase spectrophotometry), Floating Leaf Disk Assay (ET₅₀ = time for 50% of disks to float in NaHCO₃), and KOH Respirometry (KOH absorbs CO₂ gas as a solid precipitate so volume decrease directly measures O₂ consumption). Negative Control: boiled enzyme/boiled seeds or glass beads (corrects for ambient temp/pressure changes). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative & Graph Analysis: Reaction Rate = ΔProduct / ΔTime (initial linear slope V₀). Michaelis-Menten Kinetics: Competitive inhibitors increase apparent K_m without changing V_max; Noncompetitive/allosteric inhibitors decrease V_max without changing K_m. Gibbs Free Energy: ΔG < 0 (exergonic/spontaneous, coupled via ATP → ADP + P_i, ΔG°' = −7.3 kcal/mol) vs. ΔG > 0 (endergonic). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Clinical & Bioenergetic Disruption Case Study: Cyanide (CN⁻) and Carbon Monoxide (CO) bind irreversibly to Complex IV (Cytochrome c oxidase) of the mitochondrial ETC, blocking electron transfer to O₂, halting proton pumping, collapsing the H⁺ gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and arresting ATP Synthase oxidative phosphorylation. Conversely, 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP) and thermogenin (UCP1) make the inner membrane permeable to H⁺, uncoupling electron transport from ATP synthesis and dissipating proton-motive force as heat.
