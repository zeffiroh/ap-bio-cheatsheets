Bioenergetics & Laws of Thermodynamics Open Systems & Entropy
  • Continuous Energy Requirement: All living systems require a constant input of free energy to maintain low internal entropy (high biological order), power anabolism (building macromolecules), drive active transport across membranes, and support homeostasis, growth, and repair.
  • Consequence of Energy Loss: If free energy input drops below metabolic heat/entropy loss → loss of internal order → failure of homeostasis → cell death.
  • First Law of Thermodynamics (Conservation of Energy): Energy cannot be created or destroyed — it can only be transferred or transformed (e.g., solar light energy → chemical bond energy in glucose → mechanical/transport work + heat).
  • Second Law of Thermodynamics (Entropy ΔS): Every energy transfer or transformation is <100% efficient and increases the entropy (disorder, S) of the universe by releasing unusable thermal energy (heat).
  • Cells Are Open Systems: Organisms continuously exchange both matter and energy with their surroundings — they decrease local internal entropy only by releasing heat and waste products (CO₂, H₂O) that increase the entropy of their environment.
Cell = Busy Factory: Requires a nonstop supply of energy where Energy Input > Energy Lost as Heat to maintain biological organization.
Never write that cells "create" or "produce" energy! Mitochondria and chloroplasts transform energy from one form to another (light or food chemical energy → ATP chemical energy).
Exergonic vs. Endergonic Reactions Free Energy (G)
Feature Exergonic Reactions Endergonic Reactions
Energy Flow Releases free energy (Products have less free energy than Reactants). Requires / absorbs free energy (Products store more free energy than Reactants).
Spontaneity & ΔG ΔG < 0 (Negative) — thermodynamically favorable / spontaneous ("downhill"). ΔG > 0 (Positive) — thermodynamically unfavorable / non-spontaneous ("uphill").
Metabolic Type Catabolic (breaking down complex molecules into simpler ones). Anabolic (building complex macromolecules from monomers).
Classic Examples Cellular respiration (ΔG = −686 kcal/mol), ATP hydrolysis (ΔG = −7.3 kcal/mol). Photosynthesis, protein synthesis, DNA replication, Na⁺/K⁺ pump active transport.
AP Exam Scope Note: You must understand free energy concepts, exergonic vs. endergonic graphs, and ATP energy coupling, but calculating the Gibbs free energy equation (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS) is NOT required on the AP exam.
ATP Hydrolysis & Energy Coupling Mechanism Phosphorylated Intermediates
  • ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) Structure: An RNA nucleotide composed of Adenine (nitrogenous base) + Ribose (5-C sugar) + a chain of 3 negatively charged phosphate groups (α, β, γ).
  • Why ATP Hydrolysis Releases High Energy: The 3 adjacent PO₄²⁻ groups experience intense electrostatic repulsion (like-charge crowding). Hydrolyzing the terminal phosphoanhydride bond (ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ + Energy) relieves this charge repulsion!
  • Energy Coupling: Cells use the exergonic hydrolysis of ATP (ΔG = −7.3 kcal/mol) to drive endergonic cellular processes (ΔG > 0) so the combined net ΔG is negative (< 0).
  • Phosphorylation Mechanism: ATP transfers its terminal phosphate (Pᵢ) to a reactant or transport protein, forming an unstable, high-energy phosphorylated intermediate that undergoes a conformational change or chemical reaction.
Active Transport & Motor Proteins: ATP hydrolysis phosphorylates the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump (driving 3 Na⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in against gradients) and powers myosin/kinesin motor steps along cytoskeleton tracks.
Sequential Metabolic Pathways & Energy Carriers Stepwise Regulation
  • Sequential Stepwise Pathways (A →E₁ B →E₂ C →E₃ D):
    • The product of one reaction serves directly as the substrate for the next enzyme in a linear or cyclical cascade.
    • Why Stepwise Pathways Evolved:
      1. Controlled Energy Release: Harvests energy in small, manageable packets rather than one explosive combustion release of heat.
      2. Increased Thermodynamic Efficiency & intermediate branching points.
      3. Precise Regulation: Allows feedback inhibition at specific rate-limiting enzymes.
  • 3 Types of Metabolic Pathways:
    • Catabolic: Break down complex molecules → release energy (e.g., glycolysis, cellular respiration).
    • Anabolic: Build complex molecules from simpler precursors → require energy (e.g., Calvin cycle, polypeptide synthesis).
    • Amphibolic: Act as crossroads serving both catabolic and anabolic roles (e.g., Krebs cycle intermediates used to synthesize amino acids).
  • Universal Energy & Electron Carriers: ATP (phosphate transfer currency) and redox coenzymes NADH, FADH₂, and NADPH (high-energy electron shuttles). Core metabolic pathways (like glycolysis) are evolutionarily conserved across Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya.

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 5, 6 & 13 (Enzyme Catalysis, Photosynthesis Leaf Disks & Respirometry): Enzyme assay (catalase/peroxidase spectrophotometry), Floating Leaf Disk Assay (ET₅₀ = time for 50% of disks to float in NaHCO₃), and KOH Respirometry (KOH absorbs CO₂ gas as a solid precipitate so volume decrease directly measures O₂ consumption). Negative Control: boiled enzyme/boiled seeds or glass beads (corrects for ambient temp/pressure changes). Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative & Graph Analysis: Reaction Rate = ΔProduct / ΔTime (initial linear slope V₀). Michaelis-Menten Kinetics: Competitive inhibitors increase apparent K_m without changing V_max; Noncompetitive/allosteric inhibitors decrease V_max without changing K_m. Gibbs Free Energy: ΔG < 0 (exergonic/spontaneous, coupled via ATP → ADP + P_i, ΔG°' = −7.3 kcal/mol) vs. ΔG > 0 (endergonic). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Clinical & Bioenergetic Disruption Case Study: Cyanide (CN⁻) and Carbon Monoxide (CO) bind irreversibly to Complex IV (Cytochrome c oxidase) of the mitochondrial ETC, blocking electron transfer to O₂, halting proton pumping, collapsing the H⁺ gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and arresting ATP Synthase oxidative phosphorylation. Conversely, 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP) and thermogenin (UCP1) make the inner membrane permeable to H⁺, uncoupling electron transport from ATP synthesis and dissipating proton-motive force as heat.
