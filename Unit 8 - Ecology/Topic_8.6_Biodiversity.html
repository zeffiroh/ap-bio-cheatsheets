1. Biodiversity & Ecosystem Stability / Resilience Functional Redundancy
  • Three Levels of Biodiversity:
    • 1. Genetic Diversity: Allelic variation within and between populations of a species (enables evolutionary adaptation).
    • 2. Species Diversity: Variety and relative abundance of species within a community (richness + evenness).
    • 3. Ecosystem Diversity: Variety of habitats, biotic communities, and ecological processes across the biosphere.
  • Why Diverse Ecosystems Are More Resilient (Functional Redundancy):
    • Ecosystems with more component parts and higher species diversity are far more resilient to environmental change (drought, disease, storms, climate shifts, human disturbance).
    • Many species perform overlapping ecological roles (nitrogen fixation, pollination, primary production, predation).
    • If one species declines due to a species-specific pathogen or stressor, other species with similar niches step in and compensate to maintain overall ecosystem function and energy flow!
  • Low Diversity = High Vulnerability (Natural vs. Artificial Ecosystems):
    • Ecosystems with few component parts (e.g., agricultural monoculture farms, Arctic tundra, isolated islands) lack functional backups; losing a single key species can trigger total food-web collapse.
High Biodiversity = Functional Redundancy = High Ecosystem Stability & Resilience.
Natural Tropical Rainforest vs. Monoculture Cornfield: A diverse rainforest with hundreds of tree species survives a beetle outbreak that kills one tree species, whereas a monoculture crop field can be completely wiped out by a single pest or blight.
Low-diversity systems (like fertilized monoculture farms) may look highly productive in the short term, but they are ecologically unstable and fragile long-term!
2. Keystone Species, Producers & Ecosystem Engineers Disproportionate Impact
Ecological Role Definition & Abundance Classic AP Biology Examples
Keystone Species Species that exerts a disproportionately large regulatory effect on community structure and diversity relative to its low biomass/abundance. • Pisaster ochraceus (Intertidal Sea Star): Preys on dominant Mytilus mussels, preventing competitive exclusion.
• Sea Otters: Keep sea urchins in check to protect kelp forests.
• Yellowstone Gray Wolves: Regulate elk populations & browsing behavior, allowing willow/aspen & beaver recovery.
Primary Producers High-biomass autotrophs at the base of the food web that capture solar/chemical energy for all higher trophic levels. Phytoplankton, grasses, trees, kelp. Without producers, bottom-up collapse eliminates all consumers!
Foundation Species / Ecosystem Engineers Organisms that physically create, modify, or maintain the habitat structure for the entire community. • Beavers (Ecosystem Engineer): Build dams that transform streams into wetland ponds.
• Reef-Building Corals & Kelp (Foundation): Build physical 3D habitat structure.

4. Component Addition/Removal & Island Biogeography Short- vs. Long-Term
  • Adding or Removing a Component from an Ecosystem:
    • Short-Term Effects: Immediate population spikes or crashes, altered foraging behavior, trophic cascades, and disrupted nutrient/energy flow.
    • Long-Term Effects: Permanent loss of native species (reduced biodiversity), evolutionary selection shifts, community restructuring into an alternative state (e.g., kelp forest → urchin barren), or total ecosystem collapse.
  • MacArthur & Wilson’s Equilibrium Theory of Island Biogeography:
    • Species richness on an island (or isolated habitat fragment) reaches an equilibrium determined by the balance between immigration rate of new species and extinction rate of established species:
    • 1. Island Size Effect: LARGER islands have higher biodiversity because they offer more resources/diverse habitats and larger populations, resulting in lower extinction rates.
    • 2. Distance from Mainland Effect: CLOSER islands to the mainland have higher biodiversity because organisms can disperse there much more easily, resulting in higher immigration rates.
    • Highest Species Richness = Large Island Close to Mainland!

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 10, 11 & 12 (Energy Dynamics, Whole-Plant Transpiration & Pillbug Animal Behavior Choice Chambers): Choice chambers testing pillbug (Armadillidium vulgare) chemotaxis/hydrotaxis/kinesis (count pillbugs on wet vs. dry filter paper every 30 s for 10 min; evaluate using χ² against 50:50 null distribution). Potometer/gravimetric transpiration across light, wind, and humidity treatments normalized per cm² leaf surface area. Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Ecological Equations: Population Growth Rate: dN/dt = B − D. Exponential Growth: dN/dt = r_max·N (J-curve). Logistic Growth: dN/dt = r_max·N·[(K − N)/K] (S-curve; maximum instantaneous growth rate dN/dt occurs at N = K/2). Simpson's Diversity Index: D_I = 1 − Σ(n / N)² (ranges from 0 to 1; higher value = higher diversity). Net Primary Productivity: NPP = GPP − R. Trophic 10% Rule: ~10% of energy transfers to the next trophic level (~90% lost as metabolic heat/respiration). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Ecological Disruption & Trophic Cascade Case Study: Removal of a keystone predator (e.g., Pisaster ochraceus sea star in rocky intertidal zones, or sea otters in kelp forests) triggers a top-down trophic cascade: prey populations (mussels or sea urchins) explode unchecked, competitively excluding other invertebrates or overgrazing kelp producers, collapsing Simpson's Diversity Index. Similarly, agricultural N/P fertilizer runoff causes Cultural Eutrophication: algal blooms block light, aerobic bacteria decompose dead algae and deplete dissolved O₂, creating hypoxic dead zones.
