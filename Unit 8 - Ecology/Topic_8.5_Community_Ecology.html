1. Community Structure & Simpson’s Diversity Index Richness + Evenness
  • Ecological Community: All interacting populations of different species living in the same habitat.
  • Two Components of Species Diversity:
    • 1. Species Richness: The total number of different species present in the community.
    • 2. Relative Abundance (Evenness): How evenly individuals are distributed among those species (high diversity requires both high richness and high evenness so no single species dominates!).
  • Simpson’s Diversity Index (AP Formula Sheet):
Diversity Index (D_I) = 1 − Σ (n / N)²   (Range: 0 to 1; Higher value = Higher Diversity!)
  • n = total number of organisms of a particular species  |  N = total number of organisms of ALL species combined.
Worked AP Simpson's Diversity Index Calculation:
A tide pool has 30 sea stars, 50 mussels, and 20 barnacles (Total N = 30 + 50 + 20 = 100):
  • (n/N)² for each species: Sea stars (30/100)² = (0.30)² = 0.09; Mussels (50/100)² = (0.50)² = 0.25; Barnacles (20/100)² = (0.20)² = 0.04.
  • Sum of squares: Σ(n/N)² = 0.09 + 0.25 + 0.04 = 0.38.
  • Simpson's Index: D_I = 1 − 0.38 = 0.62!
High species richness alone does NOT guarantee high diversity! Community A (25, 25, 25, 25 → D_I = 0.75) is far more diverse than Community B (97, 1, 1, 1 → D_I = 0.06) even though both have 4 species and 100 individuals!
2. Interspecific Interactions Matrix (+/+, +/−, +/0, −/−) Coevolution
Interaction Effect Mechanism & Classic AP Examples
Competition − / − Both species harmed competing for the same limiting resource. Drives Competitive Exclusion or Resource (Niche) Partitioning.
Predation / Herbivory + / − Predator/herbivore benefits; prey/plant is harmed. Drives coevolutionary arms races: cryptic camouflage, aposematic warning color, Batesian mimicry (harmless mimics venomous, e.g., king snake mimics coral snake), Müllerian mimicry (two unpalatable species resemble each other, e.g., cuckoo bee & yellow jacket), and plant thorns/toxins (nicotine, tannins).
Mutualism (Symbiosis) + / + Both species benefit: Mycorrhizal fungi (P/H₂O to plant roots ↔ plant sugars), Rhizobium bacteria in legume root nodules (N₂ fixation), Lichens (fungus + algae), Coral + Zooxanthellae, Pollinators + Flowers.
Commensalism (Symbiosis) + / 0 One species benefits; the other is unaffected (e.g., barnacles on whales, cattle egrets eating insects flushed by grazing buffalo, epiphytic orchids on tree branches).
Parasitism (Symbiosis) + / − Parasite derives nourishment from host (harming host without usually killing immediately): ticks/fleas (ectoparasites), tapeworms/Plasmodium (endoparasites).
3. Competitive Exclusion vs. Niche Partitioning & Cascades Gause & MacArthur
  • Competitive Exclusion Principle (G.F. Gause): Two species competing for the exact same limiting resources cannot stably coexist — the superior competitor drives the inferior one to local extinction (e.g., Paramecium aurelia outcompetes P. caudatum in mixed culture).
  • Fundamental vs. Realized Niche (Connell's Barnacles):
    • Fundamental Niche: The full range of abiotic conditions and resources a species could potentially occupy in the absence of competitors (Chthamalus across entire intertidal zone).
    • Realized Niche: The actual narrower subset of its fundamental niche a species occupies in the presence of biotic competition (Balanus restricts Chthamalus to upper rocks).
  • Niche Partitioning & Character Displacement: Natural selection favors individuals that use different microhabitats, food sizes, or feeding times, reducing competition and causing sympatric species to diverge morphologically (character displacement, e.g., Galápagos finch beak depths on shared islands).
4. Ecological Succession & Intermediate Disturbance Community Change
  • Primary Succession (No Soil Present Initially — Slow): Begins on lifeless, barren rock after lava flows, glacial retreat, or sand dunes. Pioneer species (lichens & mosses) weather rock and build the first organic soil → grasses → shrubs → climax forest.
  • Secondary Succession (Intact Soil Remains — Fast): Begins after a disturbance (wildfire, flood, abandoned farmland, clear-cutting) where nutrient-rich soil and seed banks remain intact.
  • Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis: Moderate frequency/intensity of disturbance maximizes species diversity by preventing competitively dominant species from excluding everyone else while allowing colonizers to persist.

Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Ecological Equations: Population Growth Rate: dN/dt = B − D. Exponential Growth: dN/dt = r_max·N (J-curve). Logistic Growth: dN/dt = r_max·N·[(K − N)/K] (S-curve; maximum instantaneous growth rate dN/dt occurs at N = K/2). Simpson's Diversity Index: D_I = 1 − Σ(n / N)² (ranges from 0 to 1; higher value = higher diversity). Net Primary Productivity: NPP = GPP − R. Trophic 10% Rule: ~10% of energy transfers to the next trophic level (~90% lost as metabolic heat/respiration). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Ecological Disruption & Trophic Cascade Case Study: Removal of a keystone predator (e.g., Pisaster ochraceus sea star in rocky intertidal zones, or sea otters in kelp forests) triggers a top-down trophic cascade: prey populations (mussels or sea urchins) explode unchecked, competitively excluding other invertebrates or overgrazing kelp producers, collapsing Simpson's Diversity Index. Similarly, agricultural N/P fertilizer runoff causes Cultural Eutrophication: algal blooms block light, aerobic bacteria decompose dead algae and deplete dissolved O₂, creating hypoxic dead zones.
