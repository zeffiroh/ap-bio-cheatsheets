1. Carrying Capacity (K) Is Dynamic, Not Fixed Resource Limits
  • Carrying Capacity (K): The maximum population size of a species that a specific environment can sustain indefinitely given available resources.
  • What Determines K?
    • Abiotic Limiting Factors: Sunlight, water availability, temperature, soil mineral nutrients (N, P), dissolved oxygen, and nesting/shelter space.
    • Biotic Limiting Factors: Food availability (prey/producers), interspecific competitors, predators, and pathogens.
  • Exceeding Carrying Capacity (Overshoot & Dieback):
    • If a population rises above K (N > K), resources become severely depleted → death rate (D) increases and birth rate (B) decreases → negative feedback drives the population back down toward K.
Environmental Shifts Change K: A multi-year drought reduces grass primary productivity (NPP↓), which directly lowers the carrying capacity (K) for herbivorous elk and bison in that ecosystem.
Carrying capacity does NOT mean a population stays perfectly flat! Real populations constantly fluctuate / oscillate around K due to seasonal resource changes and time lags in reproductive feedback.
2. Density-Dependent vs. Density-Independent Factors Population Regulation
Feature Density-Dependent Factors Density-Independent Factors
Definition Limiting factors whose intensity/mortality rate increases as population density increases (negative feedback regulating N around K). Limiting factors that reduce population size by the same percentage regardless of population density (cannot regulate around K).
Usual Nature Biotic interactions Abiotic weather / catastrophic disturbances

3. Visualizing Density Regulation & Predator–Prey Cycles Snowshoe Hare & Lynx
  • Predator–Prey Boom-and-Bust Cycles (Snowshoe Hare & Canada Lynx):
    • Step 1 (Prey Boom): When lynx numbers are low and winter food plants are abundant, the hare population grows rapidly.
    • Step 2 (Predator Lag Peak): Abundant hares provide high food energy for lynx → lynx survival & litter sizes increase, causing the lynx peak to lag slightly behind the hare peak.
    • Step 3 (Prey Crash): Heavy lynx predation + winter overbrowsing of willow shoots causes the hare population to crash.
    • Step 4 (Predator Crash): Starvation causes the lynx population to crash shortly after, resetting the ~10-year cycle!
Graph Reading Tip for Predator–Prey FRQs: The prey population almost always has a higher population size (due to the 10% energy rule) and peaks FIRST; the predator curve has a lower peak and shifts to the right (time lag)!

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 10, 11 & 12 (Energy Dynamics, Whole-Plant Transpiration & Pillbug Animal Behavior Choice Chambers): Choice chambers testing pillbug (Armadillidium vulgare) chemotaxis/hydrotaxis/kinesis (count pillbugs on wet vs. dry filter paper every 30 s for 10 min; evaluate using χ² against 50:50 null distribution). Potometer/gravimetric transpiration across light, wind, and humidity treatments normalized per cm² leaf surface area. Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Ecological Equations: Population Growth Rate: dN/dt = B − D. Exponential Growth: dN/dt = r_max·N (J-curve). Logistic Growth: dN/dt = r_max·N·[(K − N)/K] (S-curve; maximum instantaneous growth rate dN/dt occurs at N = K/2). Simpson's Diversity Index: D_I = 1 − Σ(n / N)² (ranges from 0 to 1; higher value = higher diversity). Net Primary Productivity: NPP = GPP − R. Trophic 10% Rule: ~10% of energy transfers to the next trophic level (~90% lost as metabolic heat/respiration). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Ecological Disruption & Trophic Cascade Case Study: Removal of a keystone predator (e.g., Pisaster ochraceus sea star in rocky intertidal zones, or sea otters in kelp forests) triggers a top-down trophic cascade: prey populations (mussels or sea urchins) explode unchecked, competitively excluding other invertebrates or overgrazing kelp producers, collapsing Simpson's Diversity Index. Similarly, agricultural N/P fertilizer runoff causes Cultural Eutrophication: algal blooms block light, aerobic bacteria decompose dead algae and deplete dissolved O₂, creating hypoxic dead zones.
