1. Behavioral & Physiological Responses to Cues Homeostasis & Fitness
  • Organisms respond to internal and external environmental stimuli through coordinated behavioral and physiological mechanisms that optimize survival, reproduction (evolutionary fitness), and homeostasis.
  • Behavioral Responses (External Actions): Rapid movement or activity changes in response to stimuli — moving to favorable microclimates, avoiding predators, foraging, migrating, hibernating, or courtship.
    • Example: Desert lizards bask on sunlit rocks when cold and retreat into cool burrows at midday.
  • Physiological Responses (Internal Adjustments): Biochemical, hormonal, or systemic changes — thermoregulation (sweating, shivering, vasodilation/vasoconstriction), osmoregulation, circadian rhythms, and acclimatization (e.g., producing more red blood cells via EPO at high altitude).
  • Innate vs. Learned Behaviors:
    • Innate Behavior: Genetically programmed, inherited, automatic ("hard-wired") behaviors performed without prior experience (e.g., Fixed Action Patterns [FAPs] like stickleback fish attacking red bellies or graylag goose egg-rolling; infant suckling reflex).
    • Learned Behavior: Modified through experience and environmental interaction; flexible (e.g., imprinting in young geese [Konrad Lorenz], spatial learning, classical/operant conditioning, social learning).
Behavior + Physiology Work Together: A human in freezing weather responds behaviorally by putting on a winter coat/huddling and physiologically by shivering and vasoconstricting peripheral skin arterioles.
Acclimatization is NOT a behavioral response and NOT evolution! Acclimatization is a reversible physiological adjustment within an individual's lifetime (e.g., growing a thicker winter coat or increasing RBC count at high altitude).
2. Animal Movement (Taxis vs. Kinesis) & Plant Responses Directed vs. Random
Response Type Mechanism & Directionality High-Yield AP Examples
Taxis Directed, oriented movement directly toward (positive taxis) or away from (negative taxis) a stimulus. • Positive Phototaxis: Moths / photosynthetic Euglena moving toward light.
• Chemotaxis: Bacterial cells swimming up a glucose concentration gradient; trout swimming upstream (rheotaxis).
Kinesis Random, undirected change in activity/turning rate in response to stimulus intensity (not oriented toward/away). • Sowbugs / Pillbugs (Isopods): Move fast and straight in dry, bright areas (high kinesis), but slow down and turn frequently in moist, dark areas → end up aggregating in damp habitats!
Plant Phototropism Directional growth of plant shoots toward light driven by Auxin (IAA) hormone redistribution. Auxin migrates from the lit side to the shaded side of the coleoptile tip → stimulates proton pumps (H⁺) that loosen cellulose walls → shaded cells elongate faster, bending shoot toward light!
Photoperiodism Plant physiological response (flowering/dormancy) to night length (darkness duration) via phytochrome photoreceptors (P_r ↔ P_fr). Short-day (long-night) plants flower only when uninterrupted darkness exceeds a critical threshold; a brief flash of red light (660 nm) interrupts night and blocks flowering (reversed by far-red 730 nm).

3. Animal Communication, Cooperation & Kin Selection IST-5.A Signals
  • 5 Signal Modalities Used to Exchange Information & Alter Behavior:
    • 1. Chemical (Pheromones): Volatile or water-borne molecules used to mark trails (ants), signal reproductive readiness (female silkworm moth bombykol), or trigger alarm (minnows). Effective day/night and over long durations.
    • 2. Auditory (Sound): Bird songs (attract mates + defend territory), whale songs, frog calls, vervet monkey predator-specific alarm calls.
    • 3. Visual: Courtship displays (peacock train, firefly bioluminescence), threat postures, and aposematic warning coloration (bright red/yellow/black in poison dart frogs/monarchs).
    • 4. Tactile (Touch): Primate social grooming, Honeybee Waggle Dance (Karl von Frisch) inside dark hives — angle relative to vertical communicates food direction relative to the sun; waggle duration communicates distance!
    • 5. Electrical: Electroreception and signaling in aquatic sharks, rays, and electric knifefish.
  • Cooperative Behavior & Altruism (Kin Selection / Inclusive Fitness):
    • Pack hunting (wolves, lions), schooling/flocking, and eusociality (bees, ants, naked mole-rats) increase group survival and foraging efficiency.
    • Altruism & Hamilton's Rule (rB > C): Self-sacrificing behavior (e.g., Belding's ground squirrel giving an alarm call that attracts predators, or sterile worker bees raising the queen's offspring) is favored by natural selection when the benefit (B) to close genetic relatives weighted by the coefficient of relatedness (r) exceeds the reproductive cost (C) to the altruist — increasing inclusive fitness!

I. Experimental Design & Required Lab Controls AP Investigations 10, 11 & 12 (Energy Dynamics, Whole-Plant Transpiration & Pillbug Animal Behavior Choice Chambers): Choice chambers testing pillbug (Armadillidium vulgare) chemotaxis/hydrotaxis/kinesis (count pillbugs on wet vs. dry filter paper every 30 s for 10 min; evaluate using χ² against 50:50 null distribution). Potometer/gravimetric transpiration across light, wind, and humidity treatments normalized per cm² leaf surface area. Null Hypothesis (H₀): State explicitly that the independent variable treatment has no statistically significant effect on the dependent variable; any observed difference is due to random sampling chance alone.
II. Quantitative Formulas & Graph Interpretation Quantitative Formulas & Ecological Equations: Population Growth Rate: dN/dt = B − D. Exponential Growth: dN/dt = r_max·N (J-curve). Logistic Growth: dN/dt = r_max·N·[(K − N)/K] (S-curve; maximum instantaneous growth rate dN/dt occurs at N = K/2). Simpson's Diversity Index: D_I = 1 − Σ(n / N)² (ranges from 0 to 1; higher value = higher diversity). Net Primary Productivity: NPP = GPP − R. Trophic 10% Rule: ~10% of energy transfers to the next trophic level (~90% lost as metabolic heat/respiration). Error Bar Rule (±2 SE_x̄): If the ±2 SE_x̄ error bars of two treatment groups do not overlap, there is a statistically significant difference between means (p < 0.05; reject H₀).
III. Pathology, Mutation & Disruption Case Study Ecological Disruption & Trophic Cascade Case Study: Removal of a keystone predator (e.g., Pisaster ochraceus sea star in rocky intertidal zones, or sea otters in kelp forests) triggers a top-down trophic cascade: prey populations (mussels or sea urchins) explode unchecked, competitively excluding other invertebrates or overgrazing kelp producers, collapsing Simpson's Diversity Index. Similarly, agricultural N/P fertilizer runoff causes Cultural Eutrophication: algal blooms block light, aerobic bacteria decompose dead algae and deplete dissolved O₂, creating hypoxic dead zones.
